# Project 10 — Disease-Associated Mutations & Protein Structure

## Overview

This project explores how disease-associated mutations can be investigated from a protein sequence and structural biology perspective.

The main focus is understanding how a change in a protein sequence may potentially influence the structure, stability, or functional environment of a protein.

## Research Question

How can sequence and structural bioinformatics be used to investigate the possible effects of disease-associated mutations on proteins?

## Computational Workflow

The project follows a structure-oriented workflow:

1. Identify a protein and its relevant sequence.
2. Identify disease-associated variants reported for the protein.
3. Map the variant position onto the protein sequence.
4. Examine the available three-dimensional protein structure.
5. Locate the affected residue within the structure.
6. Consider the local structural environment of the residue.
7. Interpret possible structural or functional consequences.
8. Document limitations and distinguish computational interpretation from experimental evidence.

## Structural Interpretation

A mutation can potentially influence a protein in several ways.

For example, a substitution may alter:

- residue size or shape
- charge
- hydrophobicity
- hydrogen-bonding interactions
- local packing
- interaction with neighbouring residues
- protein–protein interaction surfaces

Structural visualization can therefore provide a useful first step for understanding why particular variants may be biologically important.

## Sequence-to-Structure Connection

The project demonstrates the relationship between primary protein sequence and three-dimensional structure.

A residue identified from sequence-level variant information can be followed into the protein structure to determine whether it is located in a region such as:

- an active site
- a binding interface
- a buried structural region
- a surface-exposed region
- a flexible or structurally important region

This provides a bridge between sequence analysis and structural interpretation.

## Methodological Note

This project is intended as a research-oriented computational framework.

No mutation-specific experimental effect is claimed without appropriate experimental or published evidence. Structural observations are interpreted as hypotheses rather than definitive proof of pathogenic mechanisms.

## Limitations

Structural visualization alone cannot establish whether a mutation causes disease.

A stronger analysis would combine structural information with:

- experimentally validated functional data
- population-frequency information
- clinical variant databases
- conservation analysis
- protein stability predictions
- molecular dynamics simulations
- experimental validation

## What I Learned

This project helped connect sequence-level variant information with three-dimensional protein structure.

It also highlighted the importance of distinguishing between computational observations, biological hypotheses, and experimentally demonstrated effects.

## Conclusion

Disease-associated mutations can be investigated using a combination of sequence analysis and structural bioinformatics.

Mapping variants onto protein structures provides a useful way to examine their local molecular environment and generate hypotheses about possible effects on protein stability or function. However, computational structural interpretation should be treated as one component of a broader evidence-based analysis.
